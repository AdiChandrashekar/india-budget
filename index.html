Union Budget · Education

There is no single education budget

India has chased one education target since 1966: six per cent of GDP. It has never got there, and it cannot even agree how far short it falls, because three defensible definitions of the education budget give three different answers. This reads all of them from the budget documents themselves — the Union back to 2002-03, the states back to 1990-91 — and finds that the half of the bill nobody argues about is nine times the size of the half everybody does.

Reading the budget…

The disagreement

The same year, measured three ways

Ask what the Union government spends on education and the honest answer is a question back: by which definition? Each of these is published by the government, each is used in public argument, and they do not reconcile.

The benchmark

A target set in 1966, and still not agreed on

India has had one headline education target for sixty years: spend 6% of GDP. The Kothari Commission proposed it in 1966, the National Policy on Education adopted it in 1968 and repeated it in 1986, and the National Education Policy 2020 committed the centre and states to it again. The country has never reached it. What is less often said is that there is no agreement on how far short it falls — the published answers differ by almost two percentage points, because they count different things.

Who actually pays

The Union is the smaller half, by a factor of eight

Education sits on the Concurrent List, and almost every national conversation about the education budget is about the Union's share of it. On the functional account heads both tiers share, the states and union territories budget several times what the centre does — and the gap has widened, not closed.

Priority, not just quantity

Education is claiming a shrinking share of state budgets

Rupee totals always rise. The more revealing measure is how much of its own budget a government chooses to spend on education — and across all states together that share has fallen by a quarter since 2010-11. The spread between states is far wider than the change over time.

Promise against outturn

A budget estimate is not spending

Every year is published three times: a budget estimate in February, a revised estimate a year later, an audited actual the year after that. Quoting the first as though it were the last is the commonest error in writing about this money — and the gap runs both ways.

Where it sits in the accounts

Most of the ministry's money is not booked as education

The Ministry of Education's largest account head is not education. It is grants-in-aid to state governments: money handed over, and then booked as education a second time in the states' own budgets. This is why the Union's functional education figure understates what the centre funds, and why adding Union and state education budgets together double-counts.

The whole budget, at scale

Against everything else, education is a sliver

Every demand for grants in the Union budget, drawn to scale. The two education departments are picked out; everything else is the rest of the government. The share is easy to state and hard to feel until you see the area.

The shared classification

Major heads, the one language every government shares

Major heads come from the List of Major and Minor Heads of Account, used by the Union and every state alike. Head 2202 means general education in Uttar Pradesh exactly as it does in Delhi. That is what will make a state-by-state comparison possible, and it is why this pipeline keeps every figure tagged with its head.

Coverage

Read this before comparing

  • Never add Union and state education totals. Central transfers appear in both. For a combined figure the sources are the ministry's Analysis of Budgeted Expenditure on Education or the CAG's combined accounts.
  • Nominal rupees. No inflation adjustment and no share of GDP yet. Shares of total Union expenditure are computed and are safe to compare across years.
  • Union only, so far. States are the next layer and the larger half of the question.

Where the numbers come from

  • Union figures are read from indiabudget.gov.in, the Expenditure Budget and Demands for Grants, 24 editions. Every figure carries the edition, URL and sheet it came from.
  • State figures are read from the Reserve Bank of India's State Finances: A Study of Budgets e-STATES database and Statement 26.

Claims from outside the data, and their sources

Everything else on this page is computed from the budget documents above. These statements are not, so each one is attributed. Where published figures disagree, both are given rather than one being picked.

  • The 6% of GDP target. Proposed by the Education Commission of 1964-66 (the Kothari Commission), adopted in the National Policy on Education 1968, repeated in 1986, and committed to again by the National Education Policy 2020, which states the aim of reaching 6% of GDP "at the earliest".
  • 2.7% of GDP. Economic Survey 2024-25, for 2023-24, on the narrower measure of what central and state governments spend through their education departments.
  • 4.1% of GDP. PRS Legislative Research, Demand for Grants 2026-27: Education, citing World Bank data for 2022: "Centre and states together spend around 4.1% of GDP on education, lower than the recommended 6%."
  • 4.6% of GDP. The Ministry of Education's Analysis of Budgeted Expenditure on Education measure for 2020-21, which adds education and training spending by departments other than education — health, tribal affairs, labour and others.
  • Devolution rose from 32% to 42%. The Fourteenth Finance Commission's recommendation, effective from 2015-16, raising the states' share of the divisible pool of central taxes.
  • Samagra Shiksha is cost-shared 60:40. 60:40 between centre and states, 90:10 for the north-eastern and Himalayan states, and 100% central funding for union territories without a legislature, per PRS as above. The sharing pattern for its predecessor Sarva Shiksha Abhiyan was reset at the same time as the devolution change.
  • Scheme shares of the ministry's budget. Samagra Shiksha Rs 42,100 crore and PM POSHAN Rs 12,750 crore in 2026-27, per PRS as above. This pipeline reads account heads, not schemes, so scheme figures are cited rather than computed.
